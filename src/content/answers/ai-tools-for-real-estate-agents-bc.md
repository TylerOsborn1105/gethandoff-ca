The notes from the showing are still in your phone. The buyer asked a question you have answered many times. The listing description waits until the house is quiet. For an agent in BC, an AI tool is a reasonable way to get a first draft of that writing. Client names, prices, and private conversations stay out.

**The short answer:** use the tool for wording, from facts you already checked. Keep client information out. Read every line before a client or the public sees it.

*This is practical guidance, not legal advice. I checked the official pages on 13 October 2026. Your managing broker, and a lawyer if the file needs one, are the right people for a decision about a specific client.*

## What piles up after a showing?

The evening is when the writing gets done. These are the jobs a chat tool can draft. It does not know the house. You do.

- A listing description from facts you checked.
- A follow-up from your notes, with the names taken out.
- A short caption, once the photo and the facts are settled.
- A tidy version of a voice memo, for your file.

## What are these tools useful for?

The BC Financial Services Authority published its Artificial Intelligence Guideline on 22 February 2024. It says you stay fully accountable when you use AI. The guideline's examples of chat tools include ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot. The same care applies to Gemini or any other chat tool.

Copy this for a listing description. Paste only facts you have checked.

```
Draft a short listing description from these facts only.
Do not add rooms, sizes, renovations, or nearby claims that are not in the facts.
If something is missing, write [MISSING] instead of filling it in.
Plain words. No slogans.
Facts:
[paste only facts you have checked]
```

Read it against the house before it goes anywhere. A follow-up email uses the same rule: only your notes, with names, price, and motivation left out.

## What should you keep out of AI tools?

BCFSA's guideline says you should never assume that what you type is private. Some tools store it and may use it to improve the system, so confidential details can be exposed. Even with the name removed, pieces of information can sometimes be matched back to a person.

The guideline's examples of confidential client information are a person's name, address, phone number, income, and spending habits.

Keep these out, unless the client has given informed consent and you are certain the tool does not store or use what you type:

- Name, phone number, email, and home address.
- What they will pay, why they are moving, and how they are paying.
- Notes they expected you to keep private.
- Offers, strata documents, and other papers you hold for them.
- Identity documents and financial records.

## What does PIPA expect of you?

PIPA is the Personal Information Protection Act. The Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner for BC published a guide for businesses in October 2015, the 5th publication. I read it on 13 October 2026.

The guide says PIPA applies to an individual carrying on a commercial activity. Its example is a small unincorporated business. Personal information is information about an identifiable individual, such as a name, or a detail that identifies someone alongside other details.

Three rules from the guide cover a normal week:

- Use personal information only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate.
- A new use needs the person told, and needs their consent, unless the Act allows it without consent.
- Protect it with reasonable safeguards. The guide points to section 34. Match the care to how sensitive the information is.

BCFSA's guideline points to section 14 of PIPA. The use has to match what you told the person, or you need consent, unless an exception applies.

If you are unsure, leave the details out. This page is not legal advice.

## What do the BC real estate rules say?

Licensees work under the Real Estate Services Rules. The copy on BC Laws was a consolidation current to 6 October 2026. I checked it on 13 October 2026.

Section 30 says the brokerage and its related licensees must "maintain the confidentiality of information respecting the client."

Section 41 says: "A licensee must not publish real estate advertising that the licensee knows, or reasonably ought to know, contains a false or misleading statement or misrepresentation concerning real estate, a trade in real estate or the provision of real estate services."

The AI guideline uses a listing description as the example. Check it before you publish. The guideline says the same duty covers pictures. Virtual staging may be acceptable. Label photos or videos that have been altered or enhanced, so a buyer is not misled.

Section 40 says the brokerage's licensee name must appear in a prominent and easily readable way. Section 42 says you must not advertise a specific property for sale or other disposition unless the owner, or an authorized agent of the owner, has consented. The draft will not make those two checks.

Do not rely on a tool for contracts, changes to standard forms, or advice. Section 30 already says you advise a client to get independent advice outside your expertise. BCFSA's information page, published the same day, says you stay accountable for those duties when a tool helped.

If you manage rentals, the guideline warns that a screening tool can repeat bias in its training data, including ranking people differently. You stay responsible for a fair decision.

## How do you try this on one listing?

1. Write down only the facts you have checked.
2. Remove names, phone numbers, and anything about price or motivation.
3. Paste those facts into the listing prompt above.
4. Read the draft against your notes.
5. Delete any line you cannot prove.
6. Add the brokerage name so people can read it.
7. Confirm the owner has agreed the property can be advertised.
8. Label any photo or video that was altered or staged.
9. Publish it yourself, after those checks.
