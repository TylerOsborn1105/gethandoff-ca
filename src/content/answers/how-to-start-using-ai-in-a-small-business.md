If you've been hearing about AI for a few years and still aren't sure where it fits in your business, you're in good company. Most owners I talk to are in exactly the same spot. They've read the headlines, maybe opened ChatGPT once, got a generic answer, and went back to work.

That isn't a failing. Nobody sat down and showed you what it's good for in a business like yours. This guide is that sit-down, in writing.

## Start with a job, not a tool

The most common mistake is starting with the tool: "Let's get ChatGPT and see what it can do." That usually ends in a few fun experiments and nothing that sticks.

Start instead with a job you already do, over and over, that you'd happily never do again. Three questions find it fast:

1. **What do you type more than once a week?** Quotes, replies to the same customer questions, job summaries, reminder emails.
2. **What do you do after hours or on weekends that you wish you didn't?** That's usually paperwork, and it's usually the best place to start.
3. **What gets missed when you're busy?** Follow-ups, callbacks, invoices that should have gone out on Friday.

Pick the one that would feel best to get back. Not the most impressive one. The one you'd feel.

## Try it properly, for one week

Here's a simple way to test it with ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot or Google's Gemini. They all work much the same for this.

**1. Give it your business before you give it the task.** A blank chat knows nothing about you, which is why the first answers feel generic. Start with a few sentences like:

```
I run a small [type of business] in [town]. My customers are mostly [who].
I write in a friendly, direct way and keep things short.
Here's an example of a [quote / email / summary] I sent recently that I was happy with:
[paste it, with names and personal details removed]
```

**2. Then ask for the job, in plain words.**

```
Using the same style, draft a reply to this customer who's asking
whether we can move their appointment to next week:
[paste their message, names removed]
```

**3. Read it like you'd read a new employee's work.** Fix what's wrong. Then tell it what you changed and why ("too formal", "we never offer discounts over the phone"). It gets noticeably better within a few rounds.

**4. Save what worked.** Keep a note on your phone or computer with the instructions that gave you good results. That note becomes your own playbook, and it's worth more than any course.

Do this every day for a week on the same job. By Friday you'll know whether it's saving you time.

## Keep private information out while you learn

Until you've set things up properly, don't paste in anything you wouldn't put on a postcard: customer names with addresses, phone numbers, account numbers, health details, anything about payment. Replace names with "the customer" and you lose nothing while you're learning.

There's a proper way to use these tools with real customer information, and it's worth doing once you're past the testing stage. [Is ChatGPT safe for my customers' information?](/answers/is-chatgpt-safe-for-customer-information/) walks through it.

## What "working" looks like

After a week or two, ask yourself three honest questions:

- **Is it faster than doing it myself, including the checking?** If you're spending as long fixing drafts as you used to spend writing, change your instructions or pick a different job.
- **Is the quality at least as good?** You're the judge. If customers would notice a drop, it's not ready.
- **Am I still using it?** The best test there is. Anything genuinely useful becomes a habit on its own.

If the answer to all three is yes, pick the next job. Most owners find the second one goes much faster, because they've learned how to explain what they want.

## Common first jobs that tend to work

These come up again and again, in every kind of business:

- **Replies to the questions you get every week.** Hours, what's included, warranty, "when is someone coming?"
- **First drafts of quotes and proposals** from your own notes. See [How to write quotes faster with AI](/answers/write-quotes-faster-with-ai/).
- **Turning rough notes or voice memos into tidy summaries** for a customer, a staff member or yourself.
- **Rewriting something to sound right.** A tricky email to a supplier, a price increase notice, a job ad.

For more ideas, [Five admin jobs any small business can hand off this month](/answers/admin-jobs-to-hand-off/) goes through each one in detail.

## When it's worth getting help

You can get a long way on your own with the steps above. Outside help starts to make sense when:

- you've found a job that clearly works, and you want it to happen **without** you copying and pasting each time;
- you want your whole team using it the same way, not five people each doing their own thing;
- you're about to put real customer information through it and want it set up safely.

That's the point where a few hours of setup can save a few hours every week. Before that point, the most useful thing anyone can give you is a good first job and permission to ask basic questions. Most of what people think they should already know, nobody knows.
