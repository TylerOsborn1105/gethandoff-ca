The phone rings while you are booking the next person. The same questions come in all day. The reminder is unsent, and so is the recall. That is the work behind AI for clinic front desk BC, and the same work if you looked up AI for dental office BC. A chat tool can draft the wording. It should not see the chart.

**The short answer:** use the tool for the message, from facts you already give people. Keep names, health numbers, and the reason for the visit out. Read every line before it goes out.

*This is practical guidance, not legal advice. I checked the Act, the commissioner's guide, the physician privacy toolkit, and the dental recordkeeping guidelines on 30 October 2026. A lawyer, and your college if you have one, are the right people for a decision about a specific patient.*

## What piles up at the front desk?

The day is the phone, the booking screen, and the same answers.

- The phone rings while you are already booking someone, or while you are with the person at the desk.
- The same questions, all day: hours, where to park, what to bring, how to change a time, whether you are taking new patients.
- Reminder messages for people who are already booked, still sitting in a list.
- Recall messages for people who are due back, also still sitting in a list.

None of that is the clinical decision. It is the writing around the day. The writing waits until the phone stops, which it does not.

## What can a chat tool draft?

A chat tool is a reasonable way to get a first draft of that writing. You already know the hours, the instructions, and the tone. The tool does not know the patient, and it does not need to.

It can draft:

- A reminder of a booked time. You add the name and the time yourself, after you read it.
- A recall note that says it is time to book again, with no diagnosis and no treatment in the draft.
- A short answer to a question you answer all day, in the same words you already use. Hours, parking, what to bring, how to cancel.
- A note for a call you missed: you will call back, and when. No details from the chart.

Paste the kind of message, not the record. "Write a short reminder that a visit is coming up. Ask them to call if they need to change the time. No medical details." That is enough. Put the name, the day, and the time in on your own screen, after you check the booking.

If a line states a clinical fact, a price, or a promise you did not provide, delete it. The tool fills gaps so the note sounds finished. A front desk does not send a guess about someone's care.

If the missed call itself is the problem, that is a different decision from the writing. [AI receptionist for small business Canada: worth it or not?](/answers/ai-receptionist-small-business-canada/) is that question. This page stays with the messages you send.

## What patient details stay out?

The commissioner's guide says personal information is information about an identifiable individual. A health report with no name still counts if an address, a personal health number, or a phone number would identify the person.

Keep these out of the tool:

- Name, personal phone number, email, and home address.
- Personal health number, insurance numbers, and card numbers.
- The reason for the visit, the diagnosis, the treatment, medications, and anything copied from the chart.
- Photos, forms, and referral letters.
- A list of who is booked today, even with the clinical notes removed. A name plus a clinic is already a private fact.

"Someone is due for a recall visit. Ask them to call and book" is enough for a draft. The schedule is not.

The dental recordkeeping guidelines say patient information and dental records must be protected from unauthorized use or disclosure, except where the law requires it or the patient has given express consent. A chat box is not the chart, and it is not the place to find out whether a tool keeps what you type. Leave the details out.

## Which privacy law applies to a private clinic?

PIPA, the Personal Information Protection Act, SBC 2003, c. 63, applies to every organization. The copy on BC Laws was current to 27 October 2026. It does not apply where the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act applies (section 3).

The commissioner's guide (October 2015, 5th publication) lists a doctor's office under PIPA, and lists regional health authorities and hospitals as public bodies. The BC Physician Privacy Toolkit (3rd edition, August 2017) says PIPA governs a physician's private practice and other private health providers. FIPPA governs hospitals and health authorities.

The Dental Recordkeeping Guidelines (April 2013) say PIPA covers dental offices, and that dentists must comply for patient information and records. The BC College of Oral Health Professionals still posts them and says they remain applicable.

Section 34 requires reasonable security arrangements against unauthorized access, use, disclosure, and similar risks. A reminder does not need the chart. This page is not legal advice.
