A bookkeeper, a print shop, a property manager and a physio clinic don't have much in common, except the paperwork. The same few admin jobs eat hours in all of them.

Here are five that AI handles well, how to try each one yourself, and where each one goes wrong. In every case, a person still checks the work before it goes anywhere. The point is to stop doing the typing, not to stop paying attention.

## 1. Replying to the questions you get every week

**What it looks like now:** Your hours, what's included, how long something takes, whether you service a certain area, "when is someone coming?" You write a slightly different version of the same answer, over and over.

**What changes:** Each reply arrives drafted, in your own words, ready for you to check and send.

Customer replies written and ready to send.

**Try it yourself:** Write down your ten most common questions and your best answer to each. Give that list to ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini with an instruction like:

```
Here are the answers I usually give customers. When I paste in a customer
message, draft a reply using these answers, in the same friendly, short style.
If the question isn't covered here, say so instead of guessing.
```

**Watch out for:** That last line matters. Without it, AI will happily make up an answer that sounds right. Anything involving price, dates or promises gets a careful read.

## 2. Getting receipts and bills into your books

**What it looks like now:** Photos of receipts in your email, paper in your truck or your purse, and an evening each month keying them into your bookkeeping software.

**What changes:** Receipts get read automatically and land in your books, sorted. You check them instead of typing them.

Receipts that file themselves.

**Try it yourself:** Before buying anything new, check what you already pay for. QuickBooks, Xero and most bookkeeping software have receipt capture built in: you snap a photo or forward an email, and it reads the details. Tools like Dext and Hubdoc do the same thing in more depth. Many owners are paying for this and have never switched it on.

**Watch out for:** Faded receipts, handwriting and foreign currency still trip it up. Check the totals, and keep your accountant in the loop about how things are being categorized.

## 3. Keeping track of who to call back

**What it looks like now:** Nobody follows up because nobody remembers who was owed a call. Quotes go out and are never chased. Good customers go quiet and nobody notices.

**What changes:** Each morning there's a short list: quotes sent more than a week ago with no reply, customers you haven't heard from in a while, promised callbacks. The follow-ups actually happen.

A list each morning of who to call back.

**Try it yourself:** Start simple. If you keep a spreadsheet of quotes or customers, add a "last contact" date. Once a week, paste it in (with names removed or shortened) and ask:

```
Which of these haven't been contacted in more than 7 days?
For each one, draft a short, friendly follow-up message.
```

**Watch out for:** This is the job that benefits most from being set up to run on its own, so the list simply appears each morning. Doing it by hand works, but it relies on you remembering, which is the problem you started with.

## 4. Turning notes into something tidy

**What it looks like now:** Scribbled notes from a call, a site visit or a meeting, which you rewrite later into an email, a job sheet or a summary for your team.

**What changes:** You talk it through or dump your rough notes, and a clean version is waiting when you sit down.

**Try it yourself:** Record a voice memo on your phone right after the call. Most phones will transcribe it. Paste the transcript in and ask:

```
Turn this into a short summary for the customer: what we discussed,
what happens next, and anything they need to do. Keep it under 150 words.
```

Try the same notes with "a task list for my team" instead, and see which is more useful.

**Watch out for:** Names, numbers and dates, which transcription gets wrong more than anything else. Read those twice.

## 5. First drafts of anything you dread writing

**What it looks like now:** The price increase notice. The email to the supplier who keeps getting it wrong. The job ad. The reply to a bad review. You put it off for days.

**What changes:** You get a decent first draft in a minute, and editing is much easier than facing a blank page.

**Try it yourself:** Explain the situation like you would to a friend, including how you feel about it, then ask for a draft:

```
I need to tell our regular customers our prices are going up 8% on the 1st.
I don't want to sound apologetic or corporate. Write a short, plain note.
```

**Watch out for:** Drafts can come out a bit polished and generic. Add a sentence only you would write. It makes the whole thing sound like you again.

## Which one first?

Pick the one that costs you the most evenings. For most owners, that's either the weekly customer questions or the follow-ups, because both happen constantly and both cost real money when they slip.
